Stat-Mech Scheduler

A simulation-only CPU scheduler where tasks are particles with energy, the system has a temperature, and scheduling decisions are drawn from Boltzmann weights.

source ↗ All projects

The idea

Statistical mechanics describes how particles in a heat bath distribute over energy levels according to the Boltzmann distribution: low-energy states are exponentially more likely at low temperature, while at high temperature all states become roughly equally likely (maximum disorder). This package asks a playful question with a serious core: what if a CPU scheduler used that same distribution to pick the next task to run?

At low system temperature the scheduler becomes nearly deterministic, almost always picking the lowest-energy (highest-priority) task. At high temperature it randomises, giving each task a chance proportional to its Boltzmann weight. Lowering the temperature over the course of a run mimics simulated annealing — explore broadly while hot, commit to priorities while cool.

The Boltzmann distribution in one paragraph

For a system in thermal equilibrium at temperature T, the probability of finding it in a state with energy E is proportional to the Boltzmann factor exp(−E / kT), where k is Boltzmann’s constant. The full probability is that factor divided by the sum over all states (the partition function), so the values form a valid distribution that adds to one. The key intuition lives in the ratio between two states: it depends only on their energy difference and the temperature. When kT is small compared with that gap, the lower-energy state dominates overwhelmingly; when kT is large, the exponential flattens and the states are chosen almost uniformly. This is exactly the numerically-stable “softmax over −E/T” that shows up across physics, machine learning, and optimisation.

Reading it as a control knob: temperature trades off exploitation (pick the best known option) against exploration (sample widely, including poor options). That trade-off is the whole reason the distribution is useful far outside a literal gas of particles.

How this project demonstrates it

The scheduler assigns each task an energy Ei, where lower energy means higher priority. Instead of always running the top-priority task, it computes a Boltzmann weight wi = exp(−Ei / T) for every runnable task and selects one with probability wi / Σ wj. Here the constant k is folded into the units of T (effectively k = 1), so T alone controls how sharply priority is enforced.

The result is a single dial that sweeps between two familiar regimes. As T → 0 the scheduler collapses to strict priority scheduling — the classic greedy pick. As T grows large it approaches round-robin-like uniform randomness. In between, it gives lower-priority tasks a real but small chance to run, which is a natural way to reduce starvation. The sample run at T = 1.0 shows this concretely: the highest-priority task is chosen most of the time, but not always.

The mathematics

Boltzmann scheduling 
Task i has energy E_i  (lower = higher priority)

Boltzmann weight: w_i = exp(-E_i / T)

Selection probability: P_i = w_i / Σ_j w_j

System temperature T:
  Low T  → near-deterministic, picks lowest energy task
  High T → uniform random selection
  T → 0  → pure priority scheduling

Stat-mech scheduler questions

What is the Boltzmann distribution?

In statistical mechanics it gives the probability of a system occupying a state of energy E at temperature T: p is proportional to exp(−E/kT). Low-energy states are far more likely when T is small, and as T rises all states become comparably likely.

How does temperature change scheduling behaviour?

At low T the weights exp(−E/T) strongly favour the lowest-energy, highest-priority task, so selection is nearly deterministic. At high T the weights flatten out and every task gets a near-equal chance, giving exploratory, randomised scheduling.

What does this have to do with simulated annealing?

Simulated annealing starts hot — accepting many random moves to explore — then cools to become greedy. Lowering the scheduler’s temperature over time reproduces that schedule: broad exploration early, priority-driven exploitation later.

Honest caveat: the “energy” here is just an arbitrary priority score, not a physical energy in joules, and k is set to 1 so temperature is dimensionless. This is a simulation to illustrate the distribution, not a proposal to replace a real OS scheduler — production schedulers optimise for latency, fairness, and cache locality with far more constraints than a single Boltzmann weight.
